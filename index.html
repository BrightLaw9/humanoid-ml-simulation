02 / THE POLICY

A short plan,
predicted from vision.

ACT—Action Chunking with Transformers—predicts a sequence of robot actions from camera images and the current robot state.

Here, a ResNet-18 image backbone and transformer take ego and wrist RGB images, six left-arm joint angles, and gripper state. The policy outputs 50 consecutive targets, each containing six joint positions and one gripper command.

OBSERVE2 RGB views
+ 7 state values
→
PREDICTACT
50 × 7 targets
→
EXECUTEJoint targets
+ physics steps

At 25 Hz, executing 25 actions means one second before observing and predicting again; 50 actions means two seconds. These are sequential controls, not 50 candidate actions. Temporal ensembling instead combines overlapping predictions and was tested separately.

The expert uses simulated object poses and inverse kinematics to demonstrate real grasp and contact dynamics. ACT learns from the recorded images, robot state, and actions; privileged object poses are not policy inputs. Training follows ACT’s conditional variational autoencoder formulation, with a latent fixed to the prior mean at evaluation.

04 / PROBLEMS ADDRESSED

From stalled insertion
to learned recovery.

Earlier policies reached the hole but could not reliably recover when insertion failed. Targeted early-jam corrections, broader recovery examples, and phase-balanced training addressed this failure mode: the policy now demonstrates autonomous relift, realignment, and successful insertion, including seed 14002 above. This is a demonstrated recovery capability; the remaining failed trials show that reliability still needs improvement.

01

Earlier rim stalls → demonstrated autonomous recovery.

Earlier policies grasped and lifted but drifted, tilted, or pushed against the rim without recovering. The corrected policy can now lift clear, realign, and complete insertion while maintaining its grasp, as the successful jam-recovery video demonstrates.

02

Deep wedging → earlier relift demonstrations.

Deeply wedged states could leave the arm stuck trying to lift. Corrections moved to early jam detection: relift immediately, clear the rim, realign, then descend while maintaining the grasp. The deployed ACT must learn this behavior from data; evaluation has no scripted jam detector.

03

Sparse alignment supervision → phase-balanced training.

In one correction, alignment lasted 0.68s while descent lasted 16.76s. Uniform frame sampling gave the critical adjustment little weight. Limited coverage of lateral, tilted, and jammed states also left gaps. Phase-balanced sampling and broader corrections helped expose those behaviors during training.

04

Remaining work: make re-entry consistently reliable.

A later failure still stalled with only 0.37 mm lateral error and 2.13° tilt. The remaining challenge includes orientation and repeated recovery, not just moving the peg center over the hole.

05 / NEXT CHALLENGE

New peg positions.
New hole locations.

Generalize the full pick-and-insert behavior beyond familiar object placements.

The next stage varies where the peg starts and where the hole sits. Current results demonstrate local insertion and recovery; broader spatial generalization requires diverse demonstrations and evaluation on held-out locations.

  1. Map the valid workspace.Check reachability, camera visibility, collision-free approach, and gripper clearance during insertion before sampling new placements.
  2. Move one object at a time.Collect peg-only and hole-only demonstrations with jitter: each has 25 starts at 0–3 cm and 75 at 3–7 cm. Train for two combined-dataset passes at batch 8, then evaluate.
  3. Evaluate new locations and correct failures.Hold out spatial regions, report success by moving object and distance band, preserve nominal performance, and add corrections for states the policy actually encounters.
  4. Vary both objects, then expand the range.After independent variation works, move peg and hole simultaneously. Extend toward 7–15 cm within the validated workspace in a later round.
